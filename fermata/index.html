Fermata

Overture

Fermata

noun, in music. A pause held for as long as it needs.

The pause goes where you point, and stays where you leave it.

Most interfaces talk over you. Fermata waits. It is a design system made of two typefaces, one colour and a great deal of space, for work that is read slowly and used for hours. There are no boxes to fill and no icons to decode. A checkbox is a sentence you strike through. A switch is the last word of a sentence, and you choose it. Everything the interface says is set upright in a grotesque; everything you say back arrives in an italic serif, so your words are never mistaken for ours. Colour appears once, to show where you are. Motion answers you, then rests. What remains is the space between the notes, and that is where the reading happens.

A design system for type and silence
Version 0.1
September 2026

I

Principles

Five rules. Every component below follows all of them.

Silence is structure.

Space does the layout. A hairline appears only where space alone can't hold two things apart.

Type is the only ornament.

Weight, width, size, tracking and order carry every level of hierarchy. There are no icons, fills or shadows to fall back on.

Ours in roman, yours in italic.

The interface speaks upright, in Archivo. Whatever you choose or type answers in Bodoni italic, the way a score sets dolce apart from its notes.

One note of colour.

A single accent marks where you are: the current page, the chosen option, the focused field. The rest is ink and paper.

Nothing moves unless you do.

Motion answers a hand and then rests. Pointing sketches in pencil; choosing inks it in. The overture at the top is the one thing that plays by itself, once.

House rules

Sentence case. Capitals are for names and the first word. No labels in capitals, ever.

Italic is theirs. A chosen option, a typed value, a state someone set. Also musical and foreign terms, as books have always done.

Tracking follows size. Small text opens a little; display type closes in. Nothing is tracked for decoration.

Sixty-two characters. Body text holds its measure. Long lines belong to display type.

Figures that change sit still. Counters and times use tabular figures, so nothing jitters as it counts.

Real punctuation. Curly quotes, true apostrophes, and an en dash for ranges like 6–96.

    Why two faces are enough

    Every extra typeface is another voice in the room. Two is a conversation.

    The grotesque carries everything the interface says: labels, buttons, the names of things. The italic serif carries everything you say back. Nothing else is needed to tell the two apart, and nothing else is added. In the stylesheet, the whole rule is one line: .f-yours sets the expression, in italic.

    The space isn't empty. It's waiting for the reader.

    From the studio's notes, 2026

    What this rules out

    • Icons that need a legend.
    • A third face for “personality”.
    • Colour that means nothing.

    What it asks of us

    1. Write every label so it can stand alone.
    2. Set the hierarchy with size and weight first.
    3. Leave the space empty when in doubt.
